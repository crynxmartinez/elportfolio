A prospective client asked me a version of this question recently, phrased the way it usually gets phrased: "Should we just do WordPress, or do we actually need something custom?" I didn't answer it. Not because I was dodging — because the question, asked that way, doesn't have an honest answer yet. It's missing everything the answer actually depends on.

That's not a dodge I'm proud of inventing. It's just how I actually work through anything that looks like a two-option decision. The two options are rarely the real choice. The real choice is usually three or four smaller questions hiding inside the one somebody asked out loud, and the only way to answer the big one honestly is to answer the small ones first.

This piece is that process, worked through in the open, on this specific question — because it's one of the first real decisions in almost every project, and I think most of the advice floating around it is wrong in a specific, identifiable way: it answers the question as a preference instead of as a trade-off.

## The question usually gets asked backwards

Most of what's written about "custom vs. platform" starts from a conclusion and works backward to justify it. Agencies that sell custom builds will tell you platforms are limiting. Agencies that sell WordPress sites will tell you custom builds are overkill and slow to launch. Both of those are true sometimes and false other times, which means neither one is actually an answer — they're sales positions wearing the costume of advice.

I don't trust blanket rules in either direction, for the same reason I don't follow gurus or frameworks handed down from somebody who's never seen my specific client: a rule that's right for one business's situation is frequently wrong for the next one, and the only way to know which is which is to actually look at the situation in front of you before deciding anything.

So the honest starting position is: I don't know yet whether a given client needs a custom build or an existing platform, and I'm suspicious of anyone who claims to know before asking anything.

## What I actually ask before I think about platforms at all

Before "which platform," I want answers to things that have nothing to do with platforms. What's the actual goal — more calls, more qualified leads, a specific kind of client they're not currently attracting? What's already been done — is there an existing site, and if so, what's actually wrong with it versus what just looks outdated? What's missing that the business needs and doesn't have yet? And what's the timeline — is this urgent because something broke, or deliberate because they're finally ready to invest properly?

None of those questions are about WordPress or Webflow or a custom Next.js build. They're about the business. And that's deliberate, because the platform decision is downstream of the business decision, not the other way around. I'll ask these conversationally, not as an intake form, because the real answer — the thing actually driving the request — usually doesn't come out in the first sentence. It comes out once there's enough rapport that the client stops giving me the answer they think I want and starts giving me the one that's actually true. I'm not telling them what their real problem is at this stage. I'm asking questions until they tell me themselves.

## Breaking "which platform" into the smaller questions it's actually made of

Once I understand the business side, the platform question splits into pieces I can actually answer one at a time, instead of one piece I can only guess at.

**Who touches the site after launch, and how often?** A business with no in-house technical person, updating a page twice a year, has a completely different answer than a business whose marketing person wants to publish weekly and run landing page tests. The first doesn't need the flexibility a custom build offers, because nobody is going to use it. The second might be actively handicapped without it.

**What does growth actually look like for this business over the next two or three years?** Not "do you want to grow" — everyone says yes to that. Specifically: more locations, more services, a booking system, gated content, an integration with a CRM that doesn't exist yet. A site built for what the business is today, with no room for what it says it wants to become, is a bottleneck with a delay built into it.

**How much of the value is in the content, and how much is in the experience?** A site that's mostly information — hours, services, a few photos, a contact form — doesn't need the same foundation as a site where the browsing experience itself is part of the pitch, the way it was in the scroll-driven resort concept I've written about elsewhere on this site. Content-heavy sites tolerate platforms well. Experience-heavy sites tend to fight them.

**What's the actual budget, and what's it buying — the build, or the next three years of maintenance?** This is the one people skip, and it's usually the one that matters most.

Each of those is answerable on its own. Stacked together, they tell me something the original yes/no question never could.

## The criterion I actually use: flexibility, not familiarity

Here's where I think most of the public conversation about this gets it wrong. The usual framing is "custom is more powerful, platforms are more convenient" — as if the decision is a straight trade between capability and ease. I don't think that's the real axis. The real axis is flexibility, and flexibility isn't the same thing as raw capability.

A system is flexible if the workflow built on top of it can be adjusted later without that adjustment becoming a project of its own. That's the actual test I use, on this decision and basically every build-vs-buy decision I make. It's not "can this platform technically do X." Almost everything can technically do X with enough plugins, enough workarounds, enough duct tape. The question is whether doing X later creates a new bottleneck, or just gets absorbed into how the thing already works.

That reframing changes the decision more than it sounds like it should. A $15/month page builder and a fully custom codebase can both "do" the same landing page today. The difference only shows up eighteen months from now, when the business wants something the original build didn't anticipate — and one of those two systems absorbs that change gracefully, and the other one requires either an expensive workaround or accepting that the request just isn't happening.

The principle underneath all of this, for me, isn't really about websites. It's that the system itself should never be the thing standing between a business and what it's trying to do. Time is the actual constraint every business is fighting — time to respond to a market shift, time to launch the thing a competitor just launched, time to fix the thing that's quietly costing them leads. A platform or a custom build is just a tool for managing that constraint. The moment the tool itself becomes the constraint, it's failed at the one job it had, regardless of how well it was built.

## What the performance data actually shows — and what it doesn't

Because this decision gets argued about online constantly, it's worth grounding part of it in actual numbers rather than vibes, while being honest about what those numbers do and don't prove.

The [2025 Web Almanac's CMS chapter](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/cms) measured real-world Core Web Vitals pass rates across the most-used platforms on the web. [Duda was the top performer with 85% of its sites meeting the benchmarks, followed by TYPO3 CMS at 79% and Wix, which saw a significant jump from 55% to 74%.](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/cms) [WordPress remains among the lowest-ranked with a pass rate of 45%.](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/cms)

That's a real, measured gap, and it's tempting to read it as "don't build on WordPress." But that's not quite what the number says. It's a median across millions of WordPress sites, most of which are running a dozen-plus plugins, a theme nobody's audited, and years of accumulated bloat nobody's cleaned up. It's a statement about how WordPress sites tend to end up, not a ceiling on what a disciplined WordPress build can achieve. A handful of CMS platforms in that same report — the vertically integrated, more locked-down ones — consistently post better numbers, and the report's own framing points at why: [vertically integrated platforms tend to deliver more consistent Core Web Vitals, especially on mobile.](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/cms) Consistency, not ceiling, is the thing an open platform struggles with. It's not that WordPress can't be fast. It's that almost nothing is stopping it from becoming slow, and most of the installed base eventually does.

That distinction matters for how I use this data. I don't cite it to a client as "platform X is objectively worse." I cite it as evidence that an open, endlessly-extensible system needs active discipline to stay fast, and a closed, pre-optimized one gets some of that discipline for free — at the cost of flexibility elsewhere. Which trade a specific business should make still depends on everything in the previous section.

## The cost that doesn't show up until you try to leave

There's a category of cost that almost never factors into the initial decision, because it's invisible until years later: what happens if the business outgrows whatever it picked.

Neither Wix nor Squarespace is built to let you take a finished site and move it somewhere else intact. [On Wix, your site lives entirely on Wix's servers, and there's no real export option if you decide to leave, moving off the platform is closer to rebuilding from scratch than migrating.](https://onurkurtic.ca/blog/how-to-move-from-wix-or-squarespace-to-wordpress-without-losing-seo-rankings/) [Squarespace is a bit more flexible but still has no direct export tool, and your URL structure typically changes when you move, which means redirects have to be built manually rather than carried over automatically.](https://onurkurtic.ca/blog/how-to-move-from-wix-or-squarespace-to-wordpress-without-losing-seo-rankings/) That's not a flaw in either platform, exactly — it's the business model. They're selling an all-in-one system, and all-in-one systems keep your data inside the system by design.

One piece on this, breaking down what actually transfers when a business leaves a builder, lays out the honest version plainly: [text content transfers with moderate effort and often benefits from restructuring anyway, but design does not transfer and is rebuilt entirely, while URL structure must be mapped and redirected deliberately — the highest-risk element.](https://www.kalex.studio/blog/outgrown-squarespace-wix-when-to-move-custom-website) Another way of putting that: the parts of your site that feel the most "yours" — the layout, the look, the specific way pages are organized — are exactly the parts you can't take with you. What you keep is mostly raw material. What you lose is the assembly.

I don't think this makes platforms a bad choice. I think it means the decision should be made with that cost priced in up front, not discovered later as an unpleasant surprise. One line from a migration write-up I think gets this exactly right: [the lock-in tax is invisible until you try to leave.](https://graphicredesign.com/blog/wix-squarespace-lock-in) If a business is genuinely never going to outgrow a page builder, that tax never gets charged. The honest failure mode isn't picking a platform — it's picking one without anyone naming that cost out loud, so the business signs up for convenience today without knowing they've also signed up for a harder exit later.

## The maintenance burden nobody prices into the comparison

The other cost that gets left out of most "which platform" conversations is ongoing security and maintenance — and this one is almost entirely a WordPress-specific issue, because of how its plugin ecosystem works.

A report compiling Patchstack's 2026 security data found that [91% of vulnerabilities come from plugins](https://dev.to/cifi/43-wordpress-security-data-points-that-should-change-how-you-build-sites-in-2026-fjl), not WordPress core itself, and that the gap between disclosure and real-world exploitation has gotten dangerously short — [the weighted median time to first mass exploitation was five hours.](https://dev.to/cifi/43-wordpress-security-data-points-that-should-change-how-you-build-sites-in-2026-fjl) Worse, [46% of vulnerabilities had no patch available at the time of public disclosure.](https://dev.to/cifi/43-wordpress-security-data-points-that-should-change-how-you-build-sites-in-2026-fjl) That's not a reason to avoid WordPress outright — plenty of the businesses I'd recommend it to will never run the kind of plugin stack that creates this exposure. But it is a real, recurring cost that has to be weighed against the platform's upfront affordability: someone has to be responsible for watching that surface, and on most small-business WordPress sites, nobody is.

This is the kind of cost that's easy to leave out of a quote because it doesn't show up until month eight, not week one. A custom build has its own version of this cost — dependencies need updating, a framework eventually needs a migration — but the attack surface is smaller and more predictable, because there isn't a marketplace of third-party plugins, most of them unaudited, sitting on top of it. Neither option is maintenance-free. The honest comparison is "maintenance cost now priced in" versus "maintenance cost discovered later," and I'd rather a client hear the first version from me than the second version from a hacked site.

## Where I'd pick the platform anyway

None of the above means I default to custom. I don't, and I think defaulting to anything is the actual mistake here, not picking the wrong default.

I'd pick an existing platform without hesitation for a business that needs to launch fast, has no appetite for ongoing developer involvement, and has a use case the platform was clearly built for — a service business with a handful of pages, a simple booking flow, content that won't change structurally for years. In that situation, the flexibility a custom build offers is flexibility nobody's going to use, and paying for it is paying for a feature that sits unopened. That's not efficient. It's just expensive in a different currency than the one people usually worry about.

I'd also pick a platform for a business whose real constraint is budget, honestly assessed — not "budget" as an excuse to avoid a harder conversation, but an actual number that can't stretch to a custom build without compromising something else that matters more right now. A decent site on a solid platform, live and working, beats a perfect custom build that never gets built because the budget ran out halfway through.

## When a client insists on something I think is wrong

Sometimes a client arrives having already decided — they want WordPress because a friend's business uses it, or they want a specific no-code builder because someone on their team saw a demo. If I think that's the wrong call for what they've actually described needing, I don't override it. I ask how they arrived at it.

That's not a stalling tactic. It's usually genuinely informative — the answer tells me what they're actually optimizing for, which is frequently different from what they've said out loud. "A friend's business uses it and it was easy" tells me they're optimizing for low risk and familiarity, which is a legitimate thing to optimize for. Once I understand that, I can acknowledge it honestly and then widen the menu rather than argue against their first pick: here's what that option does well, here's specifically where it might create friction given what you told me about growth or maintenance, here's an alternative that solves for the same thing you're actually worried about. The decision stays theirs. My job is making sure it's an informed one, not a replaced one.

## The actual tie-breaker, when two options both genuinely work

Occasionally the analysis above doesn't produce a clear winner — a platform and a custom build both genuinely solve the stated problem, and the business profile doesn't obviously favor one. That happens more than people expect, and it's worth being honest about rather than forcing a confident answer where the evidence doesn't support one.

When that's the real situation, I don't default to whichever option is faster to ship, and I don't default to whichever option is more common. Both of those are tempting shortcuts, and both answer a different question than the one that actually matters. The tie-breaker I use is the long-run effect of each choice — not "which gets this client live sooner," but "three years from now, which decision looks like it aged well." A slightly slower launch that leaves room to grow usually beats a slightly faster one that has to be redone. That's not true every time — sometimes slower-but-flexible is genuinely the wrong trade, if the business will never use the flexibility — but it's the actual question I'm weighing, not a rule I apply blindly.

## Where this actually leaves a client

If there's a single takeaway here, it's that "custom vs. platform" was never really the question. The question underneath it is always some version of: what does this specific business need to be able to do, now and later, and which system lets it do that without that system becoming the thing standing in the way?

That's a harder question to answer in a sales pitch than "we only build custom" or "WordPress does everything you need." It's also the only version of the question that actually produces the right answer for a specific business instead of a convenient one for whoever's selling the service. I'd rather spend the extra time asking it properly than hand someone a fast answer that turns into their bottleneck two years from now — because at that point, the system isn't the only thing that's stuck. Their business is too.
