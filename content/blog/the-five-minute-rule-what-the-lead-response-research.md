Every agency deck selling lead generation eventually puts up the same slide. It says something like "leads contacted within 5 minutes are 100x more likely to convert," attributes it to Harvard, and moves on to the next slide before anyone can ask where the number actually came from. It's one of the most repeated statistics in marketing, which also makes it one of the least examined. Nobody double-checks a stat that's already been slide-deck-approved fifteen thousand times.

That's worth fixing, and not as a pedantic exercise in citation-chasing. The actual research behind the "five-minute rule" says something more specific, more useful, and in some places more damning than the slide-deck version — and it says it to exactly the kind of business this site writes for: one that's already spending real money on a website and on SEO to generate the lead, without ever auditing what happens to that lead in the twenty minutes after it arrives. If the research is right, that gap is where a meaningful share of the return on that spend is quietly leaking out.

## The stat that's in every lead-gen deck

The claim shows up almost verbatim across hundreds of sales and marketing blogs: contact a lead within five minutes, and you're roughly a hundred times more likely to reach them than if you wait thirty, with qualification odds dropping by a similar order of magnitude as the clock runs. It's real. [The Lead Response Management Study, led by Dr. James Oldroyd and published in 2007, analyzed more than 15,000 leads across six companies and found that the odds of reaching a lead called within five minutes were 100 times higher than at 30 minutes, while the odds of qualifying it were 21 times higher.](https://voiso.com/articles/lead-response-time-metrics/) That's a genuinely striking finding, and it's old enough now — closing in on two decades — that it's survived long enough to be worth re-checking rather than just re-quoting.

The problem isn't that the number is fabricated. It's that almost nobody repeating it today is pointing at the actual study, and the study's own caveats tend to vanish on the way from the original research to the hundredth blog post about it.

## What the 2007 study actually was

Start with the scale, because it's smaller than the way it gets cited implies. This wasn't a sweeping cross-industry audit — it was [an analysis of more than 15,000 leads across six companies](https://voiso.com/articles/lead-response-time-metrics/), with the contact and qualification multipliers calculated from pooling call-log data across that group. One analysis of the underlying research is blunt about what that pooling obscures: [Oldroyd found the clear patterns "only when data from several companies is combined," and said they "vary significantly from company to company."](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/speed-to-lead-statistics/)

That's an important qualifier, and it rarely survives the retelling. The 100x figure is an aggregate pattern across six specific companies' call logs, not a fixed constant that applies identically to a plumbing company in Ohio, a SaaS trial signup, and a law firm's contact form. The direction of the effect — faster contact, better odds — is the durable part. The specific multiplier is a snapshot of one dataset, not a physical law.

There's a second thing worth separating out here, flagged well by one operational breakdown of the research: [this is a systems benchmark, not a rep-effort benchmark. If a team can't consistently hit five minutes, the diagnostic question isn't whether reps are working hard enough — it's whether a lead reaches an available rep, with full context, inside that window at all. In most misses, the cause sits in routing logic, not effort.](https://www.leadangel.com/blog/operations/lead-response-time/) That reframes the whole finding usefully. It's not really a study about salesperson hustle. It's a study about whether the infrastructure between a lead arriving and a human responding to it even exists.

## The study everyone thinks said this — and didn't

Here's where the citation chain actually breaks, and it's a genuinely interesting case of how a real statistic gets misattributed until the misattribution becomes more famous than the source. The 2007 Oldroyd/InsideSales study is not the Harvard Business Review study. There is a separate, later, Harvard Business Review article — same lead author, different data, different findings — and it is routinely credited with numbers it never produced.

[Harvard Business Review's own research, published in its March 2011 issue, opens by stating plainly that most companies are not responding nearly fast enough to online queries.](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads) The actual findings from that article are specific and, frankly, worse than the folklore version in a different way: [although 37% of audited companies responded to a test lead within an hour, and 16% responded within one to 24 hours, 24% took more than 24 hours, and 23% of the companies never responded at all — with the average response time, among companies that responded within 30 days, running to 42 hours.](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Kristina-Mcelheran/publication/298137032_The_short_life_of_online_sales_leads/links/5771477608ae0b3a3b7d5d49/The-short-life-of-online-sales-leads.pdf) The article's own conversion finding is separate from the 100x/21x figure entirely: [firms attempting contact within an hour of an inquiry were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that tried an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as those waiting 24 hours or more.](https://voiso.com/articles/lead-response-time-metrics/)

Notice what's missing from that list: five minutes. One analysis that went back and counted puts it plainly — [the HBR article, about 430 words by our count, never mentions five minutes.](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/speed-to-lead-statistics/) The five-minute, 100x figure belongs to the 2007 Oldroyd/InsideSales study. The 42-hour average and the one-hour, 7x figure belong to the 2011 Harvard Business Review article. They get merged into a single "Harvard's five-minute rule" in almost every retelling, and that merge isn't a harmless rounding error — it's two different datasets, measuring two different things, stitched together because the headline sounded better combined.

## How the mix-up actually happened

This is worth sitting with for a second, because the mechanism of the confusion is itself informative about how industry "facts" get built. [Three of the authors overlap and the two studies share a title family, which is the root of the confusion: Dr. James Oldroyd led the 2007 MIT/InsideSales behavioral work, and Oldroyd was also a co-author of the 2011 Harvard Business Review article "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads."](https://ainora.lt/blog/lead-response-time-statistics-every-study-2026) One author, two studies, four years apart, overlapping subject matter, and a publisher — Harvard Business Review — with enough institutional authority that citing it makes any number sound more rigorous than citing "an industry vendor's internal call-log analysis," even when the vendor's number is the real one.

That's not a conspiracy. It's just how citation drift works in practice. Someone wrote a sales blog post in, say, 2014, half-remembered which study said what, credited the catchier number to the more prestigious source, and every post written since has copied the previous post instead of the primary source. Fifteen years later, the wrong attribution has more search results behind it than the right one.

The reason this matters beyond pedantry: once a stat is detached from its actual study, its caveats detach with it. Nobody repeating "Harvard's five-minute rule" is also repeating "measured across six companies, with the pattern varying significantly by company" — because that caveat was never part of the version they read. The myth that survives is the clean one, not the accurate one, and that's a pattern worth noticing any time a statistic sounds a little too tidy.

## What actually held up, independently

None of this means the underlying claim — speed matters, a lot — is wrong. Later, independent work keeps landing on the same direction of effect, even with different numbers attached. [Drift's Lead Response Survey, published in 2017, tested 433 B2B companies by submitting real lead and demo forms and found that only 7% responded within 5 minutes, while 55% did not respond within 5 business days.](https://ainora.lt/blog/lead-response-time-statistics-every-study-2026) That's not a conversion study — it's a measurement of how badly the industry fails its own stated priority, which is its own kind of evidence. [InsideSales.com's 2021 research, covering 55 million sales activities across 5.7 million inbound leads, found that conversion rates drop by 8 times after the first five minutes.](https://www.engagebay.com/blog/lead-response-time) Different sample, different decade, same cliff.

A more recent, more careful read of the overall body of work lands on a fair middle position: [the five-minute rule is still directionally valid — five-minute contact converts roughly 9x better than a next-day reply — but it is incomplete as a strategy, because it describes connect probability, not conversation quality; speed raises the chance someone picks up, but it does nothing for what the rep knows when they do.](https://getperspective.ai/blog/lead-response-time-2026-benchmarks-and-what-actually-converts) That's the honest synthesis: the effect is real, the specific multiplier is less load-bearing than the direction, and speed is necessary without being sufficient.

## The number that doesn't survive scrutiny at all

There's one figure worth flagging specifically because it illustrates how far this drift can go before anyone notices: the claim that "78% of customers buy from the first company to respond." It's everywhere — it shows up in sales blogs, speed-to-lead infographics, and cold-outreach pitch decks, almost always presented as settled fact. One close audit of the underlying research found something different: [the real number closest to the 78% figure measures who the buyer contacted first, not who replied first](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/speed-to-lead-statistics/) — a meaningfully different claim, since "contacted first" and "replied first" aren't the same event, and conflating them turns a modest, plausible finding into a much more dramatic-sounding one. It's a small case study in exactly the mechanism described above: a real, narrower number gets rounded up into a cleaner, scarier one, and the cleaner version is the one that survives.

## Why this is actually a website-and-SEO problem, not just a sales problem

Here's the part that doesn't usually make it into the sales-team version of this conversation, and it's the part that matters most for a business that's already paying for a ranked, fast, well-designed website. The lead response research assumes the lead exists and is sitting in someone's inbox or CRM. For a huge share of local service businesses, the lead isn't a form submission at all — it's a phone call, generated directly by the local pack listing, the Google Business Profile, or the site's own call-tracking number. And the data on what happens to those calls is its own, separate problem.

[Invoca, which processes inbound call data for service businesses across North America, has published benchmark analysis showing that small-business answer rates fall well below enterprise norms — an estimated 20–28% of inbound calls during business hours go unanswered, a figure that climbs further in early-morning, evening, and weekend windows when owner-operators are on job sites or off shift.](https://aiclientbuilder.com/stats/missed-call-rates) An older but larger-scale look at the same question found a similar pattern at bigger volume: [a 2014 call analysis by Marchex, which examined over 20 million inbound calls to local service businesses, found that roughly 1 in 5 calls went unanswered across their platform.](https://aiclientbuilder.com/stats/missed-call-rates)

Put those two bodies of research side by side and the actual shape of the problem comes into focus. A business can do everything right upstream — rank in the local pack, win the click, load the page fast enough to hold a mobile visitor's attention, present real photography and real trust signals — and still lose a fifth to a quarter of the resulting phone calls simply because nobody was available to pick up. The lead response literature says the cost of a slow reply compounds by the minute. The missed-call data says a meaningful share of local-service leads never even get a first reply to be slow. That's not a weaker version of the problem — it's a harder one, because a missed call doesn't sit in a CRM waiting to be triaged. It just ends. The caller moves to the next search result, and the business never knows the lead existed.

This is precisely the kind of gap that a website-and-SEO engagement, as usually scoped, has no mechanism to catch. The project ends at the click, or at the phone ringing. What happens after the phone rings is operational, not digital — and because it's operational, it almost never shows up in the reporting a client actually sees. A rank-tracking dashboard will show the keyword holding position 3 in the local pack. It will not show that six of the calls that position generated last month rang out.

## What should actually get measured

If the research above is right, the metric worth adding to any local SEO or lead-gen reporting isn't another ranking checkpoint — it's time-to-first-contact, measured on the actual lead, not estimated from policy. That means timestamping every form submission against the first outbound reply, and timestamping every missed call against whether a callback happened and how fast. Most businesses can tell you their average star rating within a point of accuracy. Very few can tell you their median response time to a web lead, and almost none can tell you their missed-call rate, because nobody has ever been asked to track it.

There's a second layer worth adding on top of raw speed, flagged in one of the more careful recent reads of this research: [buyers now move across roughly ten channels in a purchase and expect to be met in whichever one they're in, which means a single-channel five-minute callback is not the universal answer it was in 2011.](https://getperspective.ai/blog/lead-response-time-2026-benchmarks-and-what-actually-converts) A business that nails phone response time but ignores the contact form, or nails the contact form but never checks the Google Business Profile messaging inbox, has only solved a fraction of its own version of this problem. The honest version of "fast response" today spans however many channels a lead might actually arrive through, not just the one that's easiest to staff.

None of this requires an enterprise CRM. For most of the businesses this kind of problem actually hits hardest — single-location service businesses, small professional practices — it requires something closer to a simple rule everyone agrees to and a lightweight way to check whether it's being followed: a shared inbox that logs timestamps, a missed-call text-back system, an after-hours routing plan that doesn't depend on whoever happens to have their phone nearby. The technology here is almost never the hard part. Having anyone look at the number honestly is the hard part.

## Where my own read of this could be wrong

It's worth being honest about the limits of this analysis rather than presenting it as settled. The foundational research here is old — the core 2007 study and the 2011 follow-up both predate smartphones being the default way people search for local services, predate text-first communication being normal, and predate most of the AI-driven call-answering tools now marketed directly at the problem they describe. It's reasonable to ask whether contact and qualification odds decay the same way today, when a prospect expects instant acknowledgment by default, as they did when the comparison point was "did anyone call back within 24 hours."

It's also worth noting that both foundational studies were done primarily on B2B and financial-services-style inbound leads — software demos, insurance quotes, loan applications — not on the home-service, trade, and local-business categories this analysis leans on for the missed-call section. The decay curve for a software trial request and the decay curve for someone who needs a plumber in the next hour are plausibly different shapes, even if they point the same direction. I'm inferring that the mechanism generalizes across categories because the underlying psychology — comparison shopping, moving to the next option when ignored — seems similar. That's a reasoned inference, not something either study actually tested.

And the missed-call figures cited above, while coming from companies that process real call data as part of their business, are vendor-published benchmarks rather than peer-reviewed research, which means they carry whatever incentive a company has to make the problem they sell a solution for look large. The direction of the finding — meaningful miss rates, concentrated around off-hours and single-operator businesses — is corroborated across more than one source here, which is reassuring, but the precise percentages should be read as directional rather than exact.

## Where this actually leaves a business already investing in a website and SEO

The headline version of the five-minute rule is wrong in its details and right in its conclusion, which is a strange but common place for a statistic to land. The specific multiplier doesn't matter nearly as much as the shape of the curve: every study referenced here, regardless of which numbers it reports, finds the same basic pattern — contact odds fall fast, and most businesses are far slower than they believe themselves to be.

For a business already paying for a ranked, fast, well-designed site, that finding should reframe where the next dollar of attention goes. The website and the SEO strategy exist to generate a lead that wouldn't otherwise exist — a call, a form, a message. Whether that lead becomes revenue is decided somewhere the website has no visibility into: a missed call during a job site, a contact form sitting unread in an inbox nobody checks on weekends, a message that gets a reply two days later instead of twenty minutes. None of that shows up in a rank-tracking report. All of it shows up in the revenue the marketing was supposed to produce and quietly didn't.
