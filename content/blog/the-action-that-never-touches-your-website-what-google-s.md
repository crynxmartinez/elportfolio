Most businesses still read their marketing the way they read it in 2019: open the website analytics, check whether traffic is up or down, and treat that number as the verdict on whether "SEO is working." That habit made sense when the website was genuinely the only place a prospective customer could take action after finding you in search. It doesn't make sense anymore, and the data that proves it is sitting inside a dashboard most business owners glance at once a month without reading closely — the Google Business Profile performance report.

That report counts three distinct things a person can do after finding a listing: click through to the website, request directions, or call. For a long time, the website click was treated as the only one that mattered, mostly because it was the only one that showed up somewhere else — in Google Analytics, in a CRM, in a form submission count. Calls and direction requests lived inside Google's own interface, disconnected from whatever system the business actually used to track leads. That's not a neutral gap. It's a blind spot, and it's one that recent data shows is getting bigger, not smaller.

## What a Google Business Profile Actually Counts

Before interpreting any of the numbers below, it's worth being precise about what Google is actually measuring, because the terminology gets flattened in most marketing conversations. A Google Business Profile tracks views — how many people saw the listing on Search or Maps — and then, separately, actions: specifically, whether someone clicked through to the website, requested directions, or tapped to call. [BrightLocal's own explanation of these insights](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/google-business-profile-insights/) describes the report as showing [what search queries triggered an impression of your listing and the types of interaction searchers have had with your listing — clicking through to your website, calling you, or requesting directions, for example](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/google-business-profile-insights/). Those are the only three conversion paths the profile itself can measure. Everything that happens after — whether the call turned into a booked job, whether the drive-over turned into a sale — lives entirely outside this data, which matters for how much weight to put on any of it.

This is the structural reason the website-click number became the default metric of success: it was the one action that connected to a system businesses already trusted. Calls and directions happened, but they happened inside Google's walled garden, invisible to anyone not specifically checking the profile dashboard. A business could be generating real volume through the profile and see none of it reflected anywhere else it was looking.

## The Numbers, and Why They Don't Fully Agree With Each Other

Here is where the research gets genuinely interesting, and where a little skepticism pays off. If you search for "Google Business Profile statistics," you'll find a split of actions cited with real confidence — except the specific split changes depending on which page you land on. One widely cited breakdown puts website visits at 34 percent of actions, direction requests at 27 percent, and calls at 16 percent. Another puts website visits at 56 percent, calls at 24 percent, and direction requests at 20 percent. A third puts website visits at 48 percent, calls at 21 percent, and direction requests at 9 percent.

These aren't small rounding differences. They're different pictures of the same supposed data point, and the likely explanation is less dramatic than it sounds: a large share of this circulating data traces back to one foundational study. As one careful breakdown of the sourcing notes, [BrightLocal's benchmark figures](https://sunnypatel.co.uk/blog/local-seo-statistics/) on the average profile's monthly actions come from [BrightLocal's Google My Business Insights Study, published July 2019, analysing September 2017 to December 2018 listing data](https://sunnypatel.co.uk/blog/local-seo-statistics/). That's a real study, run on real data — but it's data from 2017 and 2018, not 2026, and plenty of pages publishing "2026 statistics" are quietly recirculating those seven-year-old numbers with a current-year label attached, sometimes alongside newer figures from other studies, which is almost certainly where the inconsistency comes from. The underlying message survives the confusion: website clicks are not the majority or the only meaningful action on a typical profile, and have never been. But the precise split is less settled than the confident citations suggest, which is itself worth knowing before you benchmark your own numbers against someone else's headline stat.

What does hold up across the original BrightLocal research itself is the order of magnitude and the responsiveness to effort. [BrightLocal's Google My Business Insights Study](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/google-my-business-insights-study/) found that [businesses with more than 100 images on GMB get 520% more calls, 2717% more direction requests, and 1,065% more website clicks than the average business](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/google-my-business-insights-study/). Notice what's largest in that list: direction requests respond far more dramatically to a stronger profile than either calls or website clicks do. That's a real signal about where the upside actually lives, even if the exact baseline percentages are contested.

## The Quarter Where the Trend Became Visible

Averages and studies are useful, but a documented trend over time is more useful, because it shows direction rather than just a snapshot. One direct year-over-year comparison of US profiles, drawn from a sample in the five-to-ten-thousand range, gives exactly that. Comparing the second quarter of 2026 against the same quarter in 2025, the analysis found that [calls fell 11.9 percent, website clicks fell 12.5 percent, and direction requests rose 21.1 percent](https://www.savvydealer.com/blog/google-business-profile-calls-down-directions-up-dealers). The same source notes the shift wasn't a one-quarter blip: the [rechecked Q1 2026 comparison, drawn across 5,000 to 10,000 US profiles, came out at website clicks and calls each down 15.8 percent, with direction requests up 31.3 percent](https://www.savvydealer.com/blog/google-business-profile-calls-down-directions-up-dealers), meaning the pattern was present and even slightly steeper the quarter before, then decelerating.

That's two of three tracked actions falling at the same time, by similar margins, while the third rises sharply. If you were only watching calls and website clicks — the two numbers most businesses actually export into a spreadsheet or report on monthly — this would read as a straightforward decline. And that's precisely the trap the same analysis names directly: [your profile report will look like a loss and will not be one. A store reading a monthly Business Profile summary this fall is going to see calls and website clicks each down roughly an eighth against last year, with no ranking change to blame it on.](https://www.savvydealer.com/blog/google-business-profile-calls-down-directions-up-dealers) The honest reading requires adding the third number back in, at which point a declining-looking channel is actually producing more total high-intent behavior than it was a year earlier — just distributed differently across the three actions Google tracks.

## What the Shift Toward Directions Actually Means — And Doesn't

It's worth being careful here about what this shift does and doesn't prove, because the temptation is to over-explain a two-data-point trend with a tidy story. One plausible explanation is that more local searches are happening in moments where the person has already decided to go — they're already nearby, already in the car, already comparing two or three options on a phone while standing in a parking lot — and in that moment, requesting directions is a faster action than tapping through to a website or dialing a number and waiting for an answer. Another plausible explanation is that Google's own interface has shifted which action button gets the most prominent placement on mobile, nudging behavior toward whichever action is easiest to tap. A third is that more profiles now have complete address and hours information, which the photo-lift data above already shows correlates disproportionately with more direction requests specifically.

What this data does not prove is that fewer people are interested in these businesses, or that local SEO is losing effectiveness. The same report's broader framing is explicit that this is a reallocation: [your attribution is now understating the channel by design — if calls and clicks are down 12 percent and directions are up 21 percent, and your scorecard counts the first two and ignores the third, your Business Profile looks like a declining asset in a quarter where it produced more high-intent behavior, not less.](https://www.savvydealer.com/blog/google-business-profile-calls-down-directions-up-dealers) That distinction — between an asset declining and an asset simply being measured incompletely — is the entire argument of this piece, and it's easy to miss if the only number you're watching is the one that happens to match what you were already tracking before Google Business Profile existed.

## The 42 Percent: Conversion Without a Website Visit

A separate, specific figure is worth sitting with on its own, because it quantifies the blind spot directly rather than just describing it. One compilation of profile statistics reports that [around 42% of Google Business Profile conversions come from users who never visit the company website](https://newmedia.com/blog/google-business-profile-statistics). The same source found that [nearly 61% of high-performing profiles convert through calls, direction requests, or bookings rather than website clicks](https://newmedia.com/blog/google-business-profile-statistics), and that adding a booking or appointment link directly to a profile produced [roughly a 27% higher conversion rate from profile traffic](https://newmedia.com/blog/google-business-profile-statistics) compared with profiles relying on a website click to get someone to book.

That last figure is the most actionable piece of all of this. It suggests the right response to "people are converting without visiting the website" isn't mourning the lost traffic — it's building more conversion surface directly into the places people are actually taking action, specifically the profile itself: a booking link, a messaging option, service and product detail fields that one benchmark found produce [around 19% more website visits from local searchers](https://newmedia.com/blog/google-business-profile-statistics) when filled in properly, which is itself evidence that a stronger profile doesn't cannibalize the website, it feeds it. The businesses losing the most to this shift are the ones treating the profile as a static business card pointing at a website, rather than as a conversion surface in its own right.

## The Third Channel: AI Recommendations Compound the Problem

If the call-versus-click-versus-directions split inside Google's own ecosystem wasn't complicated enough, a third discovery channel is growing fast enough to change the shape of the problem again. BrightLocal's own 2026 consumer survey found that [use of ChatGPT and other generative AI tools for local recommendations has grown rapidly, rising from 6% last year to 45%, and becoming the third most popular source of business recommendations](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/). That's not a marginal shift — it's a sevenfold increase in a single year, and it places AI tools ahead of every local discovery channel except Google and Facebook.

Crucially, trust in these recommendations isn't fringe either. The same survey found [42% trust AI platforms as much as traditional reviews for local recommendations](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/), with [more users trusting them (40%) than not (32%)](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/). A separate industry benchmark of visibility in these tools found that getting recommended by ChatGPT is [30x harder to achieve than ranking in Google's local search results](https://www.brightlocal.com/resources/local-seo-statistics/) — a strong signal that the data feeding these recommendations (reviews, structured business information, consistent details across the web) is a narrower, higher-stakes target than ranking in a map pack ever was.

This matters directly to the website-versus-profile question, because AI tools draw on the same underlying signals that feed a Google Business Profile — reviews, consistent business details, structured data, real photos — rather than treating the website as a separate, primary source. A business with a strong website but a thin, inconsistent, or stale profile isn't protected from this shift; it's exposed to it twice over, once in classic local search and again in however AI assistants decide to describe it to someone who never touches Google Search at all.

## Where This Leaves the Website's Actual Job

None of this means the website has become irrelevant — it means its job inside the funnel has moved. If a meaningful share of actions never touch the website, the website isn't the place most people are deciding to take the first step. It's increasingly the place a smaller, more qualified group goes to confirm a decision they've already mostly made from the profile, the reviews, and the photos. That's a different job than "be the funnel," and it changes what actually matters on the site itself.

Real photography and specific proof matter more under this model, not less, because the visitors still reaching the website are there specifically to verify a claim the profile already made — "is this actually as good as the reviews suggest?" Consistency between what the profile says (hours, address, services, pricing signals) and what the website says matters more, because a mismatch is now more likely to be discovered by an AI tool summarizing both sources than by a human cross-referencing them manually. And speed and clarity on mobile matter just as much as ever, because the visitors who do click through from a profile are, almost by definition, already warmer and more decided than average — losing them to a slow or confusing page at that stage is a more expensive mistake than losing a cold visitor earlier in the funnel.

## What I'd Actually Change in How a Business Reads Its Own Numbers

Based on all of the above, here's the practical shift I'd make to how a business — or an agency reporting to one — reads its own marketing data each month.

**Stop reporting website clicks and calls in isolation.** Per the Q2 2026 data above, those two numbers can fall together while total high-intent activity on the same profile is actually rising. Report all three actions together, every time, or the report is structurally misleading even when every number in it is accurate.

**Treat the profile itself as a conversion surface, not a directory listing.** The booking-link data above shows a direct lift from giving people a way to act without ever reaching the website. If your profile only offers a website link and a phone number, you're relying on two of the three action types Google supports and ignoring the one most directly tied to the biggest documented photo-driven gains.

**Don't benchmark against a single percentage from an unfamiliar blog post.** Given how much circulating "2026" data turns out to trace back to a 2019 study on 2017–2018 listings, the more useful comparison is your own profile's trend over time, not someone else's averaged split of actions.

**Audit for AI visibility the same way you'd audit for search visibility.** With AI tools now a real third discovery channel per BrightLocal's 2026 survey, the signals that feed them — reviews, consistent business details, specific proof — deserve the same deliberate attention local SEO work has always given to the map pack.

## Where I Could Be Wrong

It's worth being honest about the limits of this reading. The clearest caveat is sample size and scope: the sharpest documented trend here — calls and website clicks falling together while direction requests rise — comes from a dataset of US profiles in the five-to-ten-thousand range with a particular industry concentration, not a universal, cross-industry Google disclosure. It's a real, measured pattern, but it's one analysis, over a few quarters, and it would be a mistake to treat it as a permanent law of local search rather than a documented snapshot worth checking against your own profile's numbers before assuming it applies to you.

The second caveat is about what "action" actually measures at all. As one plain explanation of the metric itself puts it, calls counted in this data are clicks on a button, not completed conversations — the dashboard, by Google's own design, counts the tap, not what happened after someone picked up. The same gap exists for direction requests: a tap to open Maps is not the same thing as a completed visit, let alone a sale. Every number in this piece inherits that limitation. The argument here isn't that any single figure is precisely right — it's that watching only the one or two numbers that happen to export cleanly into a spreadsheet, while ignoring the one that doesn't, produces a worse picture than watching all three imperfectly together.
