Search "F-pattern web design" and you'll find thousands of articles telling you to put your logo top-left, your navigation across the top, and your most important content down the left-hand rail — because that's supposedly where people's eyes go. Designers cite it like a law of physics. Agencies build templates around it. It shows up in UX bootcamp curricula as a settled fact: people read in an F shape, so design for the F shape.

That advice is backwards, and it's backwards in a way that's easy to prove, because the researchers who actually ran the study said so themselves. The F-pattern isn't a shape to design toward. It's a shape that shows up on pages that have already failed — and the correct response to finding it on your own site is to get rid of it, not to make it official.

This piece is about what the actual eye-tracking data says, what changed when researchers re-ran the same studies more than a decade later, and where the popular retelling quietly swaps "this is what bad pages produce" for "this is what good pages should look like."

## What the original study actually measured

The F-pattern comes from research Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) ran in 2006. Using eye-tracking equipment, researchers recorded how people's gaze actually moved across real web pages, rather than relying on what people said they did when asked afterward. The underlying method tracked [232 participants fitted with eye-tracking cameras as they read online texts and webpages, finding that people generally read horizontally across the top of the webpage, then in a second horizontal movement slightly lower on the page, and lastly scan vertically down the left side of the screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screen_reading). Stack enough of those gaze patterns on top of each other into a heatmap, and the aggregate shape resembles the capital letter F — two horizontal bars at the top, a long vertical stem down the left.

That's the entire finding, mechanically. It's a description of where attention clusters on a specific kind of page: one with a wall of unformatted text and nothing telling a scanning eye where to stop. It was never framed as the way people *should* read, or the way a good page *produces* reading. It was a diagnosis of what happens to attention when a page gives it nothing else to do.

## The correction nobody quotes as often as the original

Eleven years after the original study, NN/g published a follow-up specifically because the first one had been so widely misapplied. The 2017 article, titled bluntly as a correction — ["F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web: Misunderstood, But Still Relevant"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/) — exists to walk back the version of the idea that had escaped into general design folklore. It makes three points explicit that the original study never stated outright: [scanning on the web does not always take the shape of an F, there are other common scanning patterns too; the F-pattern is negative for users and businesses; and good design can prevent F-shape scanning.](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)

Read that middle point again, because it's the one that gets dropped most often in the retelling: the F-pattern is *negative*. Not neutral, not a useful default to lean into — negative, for the visitor and for the business whose page produced it. A page that generates F-shaped scanning is a page that has already lost a visitor's attention to the point where they've stopped reading and started hunting. The correct reaction to seeing that shape on your own analytics or your own heatmap tool isn't "good, that's the pattern" — it's "this page needs better subheadings, because it just told a visitor there was nothing worth reading past the first line."

The piece is also worth citing for a second reason: it's actively maintained. The article itself carries a note that it was [last reviewed Aug. 19, 2026](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/), nearly two decades after the original research. That's not a historical curiosity being left online for reference. NN/g keeps re-checking whether the underlying behavior still holds, which is a different kind of claim than "this was true once."

## F isn't the only shape — and it's the worst one

The second piece of the correction that rarely survives into blog posts and agency pitch decks: the F-pattern isn't the only way people scan a page, and when NN/g later catalogued the common patterns side by side, F came in last. A later NN/g study, ["Text Scanning Patterns: Eyetracking Evidence"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/), identified [four patterns that people use to scan text on the web — listed in increasing order, worst to best, of effectiveness: F-pattern, spotted pattern, layer-cake pattern, and commitment pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/).

The one worth understanding in detail is layer-cake, because it's the pattern a well-structured page actually produces instead of F. NN/g's dedicated article on it describes the mechanism precisely: the [layer-cake scanning pattern consists of fixations made mostly on the page's headings and subheadings, with deliberate occasional fixations on the body text in between](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/). Instead of attention collapsing into a left-hand column because nothing else on the page is distinct enough to catch it, a visitor's eyes jump heading to heading, dropping into the body text only when a heading signals it's worth the time. NN/g frames the condition for this plainly: [when headings and subheadings visually stand out on the page and are descriptive, users engage in an efficient scanning pattern that allows them to quickly find the information that they need](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/).

That's the actual lever. F-pattern and layer-cake aren't two equally valid scanning styles a visitor picks between — they're what happens to the same visitor depending on whether the page gave them real structure to navigate by. A page with vague, decorative subheadings ("Our Approach," "What We Offer," "Get Started") produces F-shaped scanning almost by default, because those headings don't tell a scanning eye anything, so attention collapses back into reading the first line of body text and hoping for the best. A page with specific, information-carrying subheadings produces layer-cake scanning, because the headings themselves are doing enough work that a visitor can navigate by them alone.

## What thirteen years of re-testing actually changed

This is where the research gets genuinely interesting for anyone trying to use it today rather than just cite it, because NN/g didn't run this study once and leave it. They repeated it, with new participants, years apart, and compared results — which gives an actual trend line instead of a single snapshot. The 2020 synthesis, ["How People Read Online: New and Old Findings"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-people-read-online/), pulled together findings across [5 eyetracking studies conducted for these editions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-people-read-online/) to see what had changed and what hadn't.

The headline finding is that the most basic behavior hasn't moved at all. As the report puts it, [people rarely read online — they're far more likely to scan than read word for word, a fundamental truth of online information-seeking behavior that hasn't changed in 23 years](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-people-read-online/). That's a genuinely strong claim: not "still true as of our last check," but unchanged across more than two decades of design trends, screen sizes, and connection speeds. Whatever is producing scanning behavior, it isn't a quirk of 2006-era web design that modern UX patterns grew out of. It's closer to a fixed constraint of how attention works under time pressure, and it's one every premium-looking redesign has to work within rather than around.

But one specific number did move, and it moved in a direction that contradicts a piece of advice almost as widely repeated as the F-pattern itself: "keep everything above the fold, because nobody scrolls." The data says the opposite happened over time. In the earlier research, [people spent 80% of their screen viewing time "above the fold"](https://www.parentcenterhub.org/web-reading/) — the area visible without scrolling. By the later study, [the time people spend above the fold is only 57%](https://www.parentcenterhub.org/web-reading/). That's a real, measured shift, not a hunch: visitors are spending notably more of their attention below the first screenful than they used to, which means a design decision justified by "nobody scrolls" is leaning on an assumption the same research organization's own numbers no longer fully support.

A second number shifted too, in the opposite direction, which is worth sitting with rather than smoothing over. Separate NN/g eye-tracking research on left-right attention found that in [a 2017 study with more than 120 participants, 80% of fixations fell on the left half of the screen and 20% on the right half, where the original 2010 study had measured 69%](https://talhaaslan.com/en/blog/f-pattern-web-design-how-users-scan-pages/). So over roughly the same window that below-the-fold attention grew, left-side bias also grew — people are scrolling further, but staying more locked to the left edge of the screen while they do it.

It would be convenient to stitch those two numbers into one tidy story — "attention is becoming simultaneously more vertical and more left-biased as screens narrow toward mobile" — and that's a plausible read. But it's worth being honest about what these two data points can't tell you on their own: they come from different studies, with different participant pools and different page sets, years apart, and NN/g doesn't present them as a single controlled trend line. Reading them as "proof mobile narrowed human attention" overstates what two separate measurements, taken for different purposes, can actually establish. The more defensible reading is narrower: both the vertical and horizontal defaults moved in the direction you'd expect if browsing behavior shifted toward narrower, more scroll-heavy screens over that decade — which is consistent with the mobile shift everyone already knows happened, rather than a surprising new discovery on its own.

## Where the data itself has limits

Since this piece is arguing that people cite this research loosely, it's only fair to apply the same scrutiny to the research itself before using it to tell anyone how to build a website.

Eye-tracking tells you where a gaze landed. It doesn't tell you what someone understood, remembered, or intended to do next — fixation isn't comprehension, and NN/g's own work is careful to frame its findings as scanning behavior, not reading comprehension outcomes. It's also worth noting that the way a study is set up can shape its own results: NN/g has published separate research specifically on this risk, noting that [the way researchers write tasks can bias usability results by influencing where people focus their attention](https://www.nngroup.com/topic/eyetracking/). If a lab study hands someone a specific task — "find the phone number" — their gaze pattern will look different than it would during open-ended, unmotivated browsing, which is closer to how a stranger actually lands on a local business's homepage from a Google search. None of this invalidates the findings. It does mean "people scan in an F shape" is a description of a particular kind of attention under particular conditions, not an immutable fact about human vision that applies identically to every page, every task, and every visitor.

It's also worth flagging, honestly, where my own read of this could be wrong. The above-the-fold and left-attention numbers above come from different studies run years apart for different purposes, and treating them as a matched pair risks implying more precision than the underlying research claims. The safer conclusion isn't "attention behaves exactly like this, provably" — it's "this is the best publicly available evidence we have, it's been re-tested rather than asserted once, and it points consistently in one direction across multiple independent checks." That's a meaningfully different, more honest claim than most of the blog posts citing this research bother to make.

## What this actually means for a real business page

None of this is abstract once it gets applied to the kind of page an actual visitor lands on — a local contractor's services page, a law firm's practice-area page, a clinic's homepage. The practical implications split into a few concrete decisions.

**Subheadings have to carry real information, not mood.** A subheading like "Our Philosophy" does nothing for a scanning eye; it's a vibe, not a signpost. A subheading like "Why We Use Reclaimed Oak Instead of Veneer" is something a layer-cake scanner can act on without reading a word underneath it — they either care about that specific thing or they don't, and they can decide in under a second which section to actually read. Per the layer-cake research above, that's the entire mechanism that pulls a visitor out of F-shaped collapse and into productive scanning.

**The first sentence under every heading should assume nobody reads the second one.** This isn't cynicism about your copy — it's a direct response to the fact that scanning, not reading, has been the dominant behavior [for 23 years running](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-people-read-online/) according to NN/g's own repeated testing. If the most important claim in a paragraph is buried in sentence three, a scanning visitor working in an F-pattern or a layer-cake pattern will both miss it — one because they stopped reading that line early, the other because they skipped the paragraph entirely once the heading didn't earn a closer look.

**Below-the-fold content is not automatically wasted.** Given that above-the-fold attention has measurably dropped from roughly four-fifths of viewing time to a little over half, per the research above, a premium site that front-loads every important claim into a cramped first screen and treats everything below it as an afterthought is solving a problem that's gotten smaller, not bigger, over the last decade. The real risk below the fold isn't that nobody gets there — it's that nothing below the fold gives them a reason to keep scanning once they do.

**Left-edge real estate earns its weight.** Given the consistent, repeated finding that attention skews left — and that the skew, if anything, grew between the 2010 and 2017 measurements — a phone number, a primary call-to-action, or a key credential sitting against the left margin of a layout is working with the data, not against it. That doesn't mean every element needs to be crammed left; it means the left edge of a layout is not a neutral place to put secondary information, the way a lot of symmetrical, centered templates default to doing.

## Why the misreading persists anyway

It's worth asking why "design in the shape of an F" survived as popular advice for nearly two decades despite the researchers who produced the data explicitly disowning that interpretation within eleven years of publishing it. Part of the answer is mundane: "design like this shape" is a far easier instruction to put in a slide deck than "diagnose whether your content structure is failing, and if it is, fix the structure rather than imitate the symptom." The corrected version requires actually auditing a page's headings and paragraph structure. The popularized version requires remembering a letter.

The other part of the answer is that the original finding is genuinely compelling as an image — a heatmap that looks like a letter is a striking, shareable visual, and striking visuals get cited long after their footnotes get dropped. The 2017 correction is less visually dramatic: there's no equally tidy single-letter shape for "these four patterns exist, and the one that matters most is the one where your headings are specific enough to be scanned on their own." It's correct, it's actionable, and it doesn't fit in a single icon.

## Where this leaves a premium website specifically

A site built to feel premium — careful pacing, real photography, a deliberate sequence of information — is, by its own design philosophy, trying to earn more than a scan. It's trying to earn attention past the first screenful, which is exactly the behavior the above-the-fold data shows is more available now than it was in 2010, not less. But that opportunity only gets captured if the structure underneath the aesthetic gives a scanning visitor something to navigate by. A beautifully designed page with vague section titles and dense, unbroken paragraphs will still collapse into F-shaped scanning — expensive design doesn't override how attention works under time pressure, it just makes the collapse look nicer while it happens.

The actual takeaway isn't a layout trick. It's a standard to hold every section of a page to: could a visitor who read only the subheadings on this page, in order, understand what the business does, what makes it different, and what to do next? If the answer is yes, the page is built for layer-cake scanning, which NN/g's own data ranks as one of the most effective patterns there is. If the answer is no, the page is one unformatted paragraph away from producing the exact shape the research has been trying to get people to stop designing for since 2017.
