The call that starts this kind of engagement almost always sounds the same. The business has been doing fine, the website had been ranking for years, and then at some point in the last few months, the phone got quieter. Nobody can point to a single cause. The traffic numbers, when anyone finally pulls them up, show a line that used to trend slowly upward and now just stops — not a gradual decline, a cliff, usually within a few weeks of a specific date. That date, almost without exception, is the week a new website went live.

This is a case study about one version of that situation — a composite built from a pattern that shows up often enough to be worth naming precisely, not a single named client. No company name, no invented traffic numbers belonging to a real business — just the mechanics, because the mechanics are what actually generalize.

The business in question was a mid-size, well-established home services contractor — the kind of operation with a decade-plus of real jobs behind it, a handful of trucks, and a website that had, for years, quietly done its job. It wasn't pretty. It ran on an older platform, the design hadn't been touched since the early 2020s, and the owner had been embarrassed by it for a while. But it ranked. Service pages for the core offerings sat on page one for the exact searches that mattered — "[service] + [city]" terms, the bread-and-butter commercial-intent queries that actually produce phone calls in this kind of business. An SEO freelancer had been managing that slowly, steadily, on a modest monthly retainer, for a few years.

Then the owner decided the site finally needed to look like the business it had become. A design-focused agency was brought in to handle the redesign. The SEO freelancer stayed on retainer, technically. Nobody explicitly removed him from the project. He just wasn't in the room when the new site was being built.

## The Website That Was Already Working

It's worth sitting with this part of the story longer than most case studies do, because it's the part that gets skipped past on the way to the dramatic rankings collapse — and it's actually the most important variable in the whole situation.

This wasn't a website that needed SEO work. It had service pages built around individual offerings, each one targeting a specific commercial query. It had years of internal links pointing between those pages and the homepage, built up slowly and organically the way real link structures tend to get built — not by a plan, but by cumulative small decisions made over time. It had a heading structure that, however unglamorous, matched what the content was actually about. None of this was impressive to look at. All of it was, in the most literal sense, working.

That distinction matters because the eventual damage wasn't a story about bad SEO getting exposed by a better-looking website. It was the opposite: a genuinely functional, if unattractive, technical foundation getting quietly dismantled by a project that was never evaluated against that foundation at all. The redesign team wasn't incompetent. They were simply solving a different problem than the one that actually existed — "make this look premium" — without anyone telling them that the old site, ugly as it was, was carrying real weight they needed to preserve.

## Why Two Vendors Felt Like the Responsible Choice

Nobody involved in this project thought they were doing anything risky. Keeping a specialist SEO freelancer on retainer while hiring a design agency for the visual rebuild is, if anything, the more careful-sounding version of this decision — it looks like due diligence, not corner-cutting. That's exactly why this pattern is worth examining instead of dismissing as an obvious mistake made by an unsophisticated business owner.

It's also, by a wide margin, the normal way this gets structured, especially at the size of business in this story. A [survey of 1,000 marketers covered by NP Digital](https://neilpatel.com/marketing-stats/seo-pr-content-brand-team-integration/) found that [among companies with less than $10 million in revenue, 44% report fully siloed teams, the highest rate across all tiers](https://neilpatel.com/marketing-stats/seo-pr-content-brand-team-integration/), and that rate drops sharply as company size increases. A business running on a handful of trucks and a modest retainer isn't an outlier for having two disconnected vendors. It's the statistical norm.

The deeper research on why this keeps happening lands on a specific, unglamorous cause: coordination itself is the actual bottleneck, not competence on either side. A [2025 Spiegel Research Center study cited by Bryck Road](https://bryckroad.com/blog/marketing-silos/) found that [44.7% of marketing executives named coordinating their marketing efforts as a top concern, more than any other area measured](https://bryckroad.com/blog/marketing-silos/), and [35.3% of mid-market firms said they hit functional silos with their vendors and agencies](https://bryckroad.com/blog/marketing-silos/). These aren't small, undisciplined operations failing to manage their vendors. These are experienced marketing leaders, at real budget levels, running into the same structural gap.

The same piece makes an observation worth repeating almost verbatim, because it describes exactly what happened in this case before anyone noticed: [the website is usually where the stitches show first — it's where every vendor's work lands at once: the design, the SEO, the ad traffic, the new logo](https://bryckroad.com/blog/marketing-silos/). A redesign isn't a neutral canvas. It's the one place where every previous decision, made by every previous vendor, either survives the transition or doesn't — and nobody was assigned to check.

## What "Just a Redesign" Actually Touched

Here's where the specifics matter, because "the redesign broke the SEO" is vague enough to be unfalsifiable, and the actual mechanism is anything but vague.

The new site consolidated what had been eight separate, narrowly-focused service pages into three broader category pages, on the reasonable-sounding design logic that a cleaner navigation menu looks more premium than a long dropdown list. Each of the eight old URLs either disappeared or redirected to a generic catch-all page instead of its specific replacement. The internal linking that had, for years, pointed relevant pages at each other was rebuilt from scratch around the new navigation, with no reference to which of the old links had actually been carrying ranking weight. Heading structure was rewritten entirely around brand voice and visual hierarchy, with no one checking it against what the pages had previously ranked for. And a handful of supporting blog posts — thin, old, unglamorous, but quietly linked-to and indexed — were removed outright as part of a "content cleanup" that was never cross-checked against Search Console.

None of these were reckless decisions in isolation. Every one of them is defensible purely as a design or content choice. That's precisely the trap: each individual change looked like good practice to the person making it, because each person was only looking at their own half of the problem.

A [checklist built specifically around auditing these failures](https://sunnypatel.co.uk/blog/website-redesign-seo-checklist/) makes the point bluntly: [the redesign itself is never the problem — it's what changes underneath that destroys rankings](https://sunnypatel.co.uk/blog/website-redesign-seo-checklist/). The same source estimates the damage by category, and the ranges track closely with what showed up here: changed URLs carrying the highest risk, with removed content and broken internal linking close behind, and even page-speed regressions capable of compounding the loss on their own.

It's also worth noting what almost got lost in the shuffle but didn't quite: the phone number's click-to-call behavior on mobile, rebuilt as part of the new header design, was nearly dropped entirely during a later round of visual polish before anyone caught it. That detail matters beyond this one project — [industry data cited by Watson Co.](https://watsonco.marketing/blog/2026/08/call-tracking-home-services/) notes that [70% to 80% of home services leads arrive by phone call, not web form](https://watsonco.marketing/blog/2026/08/call-tracking-home-services/). A redesign that treats the phone number as a styling detail rather than the primary conversion mechanism is solving the wrong problem even when every SEO signal survives intact.

## The Audit: Finding What Broke, Keyword by Keyword

Diagnosing this kind of collapse isn't mysterious, but it does require someone willing to do unglamorous, comparative work rather than guessing from the surface.

The first step was pulling the site's historical ranking and URL data from before the relaunch and setting it next to the current sitemap, line by line. This is slower than it sounds and faster than it should be to skip. Every query that used to have a specific, well-matched landing page needed to be checked against what currently answers that query — and in this case, roughly half of the previously-ranking service terms now pointed at a broad category page that barely mentioned the specific service by name.

The second step was a redirect audit — not just checking that old URLs resolved to something, but checking that they resolved to the right something. Several had been redirected to the homepage by default, the laziest and most damaging version of a redirect, because it tells Google the old page's specific relevance no longer exists anywhere on the site.

The third step was checking what had simply vanished — content, headings, internal links — against what Search Console and historical crawl data showed had actually been indexed and earning impressions before the relaunch. This is where the removed blog posts turned up, along with a batch of supporting FAQ content that had been folded into a single generic page.

None of this required exotic tooling. It required treating the old site's performance as a dataset to be reconciled against, rather than a thing to be casually replaced.

## This Isn't a Freak Occurrence

It's tempting to treat a story like this as a one-off, a particularly unlucky combination of circumstances. It isn't. This exact pattern — real traffic, real rankings, quietly erased by a redesign that nobody checked against the baseline — is documented often enough to qualify as a known failure mode, not a rare accident.

A [recovery case published by Hog the Web](https://hogtheweb.com/services/seo-recovery-after-wordpress-redesign/) describes a small, family-owned painting business that lost the overwhelming majority of its organic search traffic after a redesign by a different provider, before being rebuilt with the old ranking structure respected. The same source's broader explanation of the failure mode lists the usual suspects plainly: [deleted ranking pages, missing redirects, changed URLs, lost internal links, indexation problems, altered canonical tags, substantially reduced content and other technical changes](https://prunderground.com/hog-the-web-restores-organic-traffic-after-website-redesign-causes-97-collapse/cmtaywvf2000c04jofbq1gpev).

A separate, unrelated case involving a financial services firm shows the same pattern at a different scale. A [recovery case study covering the aftermath](https://www.godigitalglobally.com/article/how-to-fix-website-after-failed-redesign/) of a redesign found that [organic clicks declined from 428 to 64 following a website redesign](https://www.godigitalglobally.com/article/how-to-fix-website-after-failed-redesign/), and draws a conclusion worth sitting with regardless of industry: [this case demonstrates why website design alone cannot guarantee sustainable organic growth](https://www.godigitalglobally.com/article/how-to-fix-website-after-failed-redesign/).

What's notable about both of these isn't the specific percentage lost — numbers like that vary enormously by site size, authority, and what exactly broke. What's notable is that in both cases, as in the composite story here, the redesign itself wasn't visually bad. It wasn't a case of an incompetent design team producing something ugly or broken-looking. The sites looked fine. They simply stopped being connected to what Google had already decided they were about.

## What It Actually Took to Fix

Fixing this wasn't a matter of reverting to the old design — nobody wanted that, and the new site's actual visual work was, on its own terms, an improvement. The fix was reconnecting the new design to the old site's earned signals, piece by piece.

That meant rebuilding the service pages the redesign had consolidated — not as a wholesale return to eight separate URLs, but as a deliberate decision about which distinctions actually mattered for search intent and which didn't, made with both the design and the ranking history in view at the same time. It meant correcting the redirect map so that each meaningfully different old URL pointed at a meaningfully equivalent new one, rather than defaulting everything upstream to the homepage. It meant restoring the specific content — the FAQ detail, the supporting posts — that had been quietly earning impressions before being folded away, and giving it a home in the new structure rather than treating it as clutter. And it meant re-establishing a heading hierarchy that served both the visual design and the actual topical structure Google had already rewarded, instead of treating those as competing goals.

None of this required abandoning the premium redesign. It required treating the redesign as an edit to an existing, valuable asset rather than a replacement of one — the same discipline that makes renovating a building with good bones different from demolishing it and hoping the new one earns the same trust from scratch.

## The Rankings Came Back. The Conversion Problem Hadn't.

It's worth being honest about something most recovery stories skip: fixing the technical collapse brought the rankings and the traffic back, but it didn't automatically fix how well that traffic converted — because that was always a separate question, and the redesign, for all its SEO problems, had genuinely improved the visual experience.

This is where the two disciplines actually do meet, and where it's worth grounding expectations in real numbers rather than vibes. An [analysis of 41,000 landing pages](https://gravitygone.co/ai-automation/conversion-focused-landing-pages-local-services/) — [464 million visits, and 57 million conversions](https://gravitygone.co/ai-automation/conversion-focused-landing-pages-local-services/) — [found a median conversion rate of 6.6% across all industries, and treats 10% or above as genuinely good](https://gravitygone.co/ai-automation/conversion-focused-landing-pages-local-services/). That's a useful baseline for a business owner to hold the recovered site against, once the rankings themselves are no longer in question. Getting the traffic back is necessary. It isn't the same project as making sure that traffic actually picks up the phone, and conflating the two — assuming a technically sound site is automatically a converting one — is its own, quieter version of the same mistake that caused the original collapse: treating one half of the problem as the whole problem.

## What Actually Generalizes From This

The specific failure here — consolidated URLs, lost redirects, stripped content — is just the mechanism. The actual lesson sits one level up, and it's the same lesson the silo research above keeps landing on from a different angle: a website redesign and an SEO program cannot be run as two unrelated projects that happen to share a URL, because the website is the only place where both of them are forced to agree with each other or visibly fail to.

That doesn't necessarily mean one person has to personally execute both disciplines, though there's a real argument for that at smaller scale — it's a lot easier to keep two concerns coordinated inside one head than across two separate contracts, two separate invoices, and two separate ideas of what "done" looks like. What it does mean, at minimum, is that before a single URL changes, before a single page gets "cleaned up" or consolidated, someone with visibility into both the ranking history and the new structure needs to actually look at the two side by side and sign off that nothing load-bearing is being quietly removed. [The clearest sign your marketing vendors aren't aligned is reports that all look great while the business stays flat](https://bryckroad.com/blog/marketing-silos/) — and a redesign is exactly the moment where that gap stops being theoretical and starts costing real phone calls.

The business in this story didn't do anything unusual. It made the same reasonable-sounding decision that a large share of businesses its size make, for the same reasonable-sounding reasons, and ran into a well-documented, entirely preventable failure as a direct result. The fix wasn't more SEO or better design. It was making sure the two were never allowed to operate as if the other didn't exist.
