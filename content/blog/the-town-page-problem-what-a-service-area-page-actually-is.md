A plumbing company with one shop and one truck fleet decides to "do local SEO properly." Someone, somewhere, told them the move is to build a page for every town they service — twenty-two towns, twenty-two pages, each one titled "Plumber in [Town Name]." Six months later, most of those pages are sitting in Google Search Console tagged *Crawled – currently not indexed*, the handful that did get indexed rank nowhere, and the one page that actually brings in calls is the homepage, the same page that was already there before any of this started.

This is not a rare outcome. It's close to the default outcome, because the thing that was built wasn't twenty-two service-area pages. It was one page, copy-pasted twenty-two times with the city name swapped, which is a different object with a name of its own: a doorway page. Understanding the difference between those two things — a real service-area page and a doorway page wearing one as a costume — is the entire subject of this piece, because it's a distinction almost nobody explains clearly before a business spends real money building the wrong one.

## What a service-area page actually is

Start with the plain definition, because the term gets used loosely. A service-area page is a page on your website built for a specific place you serve, aimed at someone searching something like "[service] in [town]," that exists to answer a real question: *what is it actually like to hire this business, for this job, in this specific place?*

That's the whole brief. Not "rank for the city name." Answer the actual question a person in that town has, which usually includes things like how fast you can get there, whether you've done work in that specific area before, what's different about that market (older housing stock, a specific permitting office, a climate quirk, a drive-time consideration), and proof — a finished job, a review, a specific number — that you're not guessing about any of it.

It's worth being precise about what this is *not*, because the confusion starts here. A service-area page is not the same thing as the service-area setting inside your Google Business Profile. Those are two different systems solving two different problems, and conflating them is one of the most common setup mistakes a business makes.

## The two systems that get mixed up

The Google Business Profile service-area feature is a configuration setting, not content. It tells Google's local-pack and Maps algorithm which geography your one profile should be eligible to show up in. Google's own documentation on this is specific: you can set [up to 20 service areas](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en), and [the boundaries of your overall area shouldn't be more than about 2 hours of driving time from where your business is based](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en). That's the entire function of the field — it's a boundary you draw once, in a dashboard, that shapes which map-pack searches you're even in the running for.

The service-area *page*, by contrast, is a page on your actual website, competing in ordinary organic search results, not the map pack. It has to carry its own weight the way any page does — unique content, real information, something worth a click and a read. The GBP setting is a lever you pull once. The page is something you have to actually write, and write well, for every town you claim one.

Where this gets businesses into trouble is assuming that setting the GBP field correctly is equivalent to doing the website work. It isn't. You can have a perfectly configured service area in your profile and a set of website pages that actively work against you, because the profile setting and the page content are evaluated by completely different parts of Google's systems, for completely different purposes.

It's also worth knowing what counts as a service-area business in the first place, because the designation changes what you're allowed to show. Google's definition is specific: [a business that visits or delivers to customers directly but doesn't serve customers at their business address](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en) — [businesses like cleaning services or plumbers](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en) are the textbook example. And the guidance on address visibility is direct, not a suggestion: [if you don't serve customers at your business address, remove your address from your Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en). A hybrid business — one with a real storefront that also travels to customers — is allowed to show both. A pure service-area business showing a home address it doesn't actually receive anyone at is creating exactly the kind of mismatch between claim and reality that makes a profile look, and eventually get treated as, unreliable.

## Why this exists at all: the actual business problem

None of this matters in the abstract. It matters because of a specific, common situation: a business with one location — one office, one crew, one phone number — legitimately works across a dozen or more towns, and almost none of those towns will ever type the business's actual name into Google. They'll type "[service] [their town]," because that's the honest shape of how someone searches for a local tradesperson, contractor, or specialist they've never heard of.

If the only page answering that search is a generic homepage with no mention of the town at all, the business is invisible to that search, even though they genuinely do the work there. A service-area page exists to close exactly that gap — to give Google, and the person searching, a real answer that happens to be specific to the place they typed.

That's a legitimate need. The problem isn't the idea. The problem is almost every guide on the internet tells a business to solve it by building one page per town, without explaining that the quality bar for each of those pages is the same as the quality bar for any other page competing in search — and that quality bar is exactly where most of these projects quietly fail.

## Where it goes wrong: Google's actual definition of a doorway page

Google doesn't describe this as a gray area. The company's current spam policy documentation names the pattern directly. [Doorway abuse is when sites or pages are created to rank for specific, similar search queries, leading users to intermediate pages that aren't as useful as the final destination.](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) The documentation goes on to list the exact pattern at issue: [having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) is named explicitly as one of the behaviors the policy covers.

Read that definition against a typical set of twenty-two swapped-city-name pages and the match is uncomfortable. Each page targets a specific query. Each one leads to the same intermediate experience — a contact form, a phone number, maybe a paragraph with the town name dropped into three sentences that are otherwise identical to the page one town over. Nobody reading that page learns anything they couldn't have learned from the homepage. That's not a stylistic complaint; it's the literal pattern the policy describes.

This isn't a new or obscure concern, either — it predates the current documentation by over a decade. The idea of a doorway page, as a category, traces back to [web pages created for the deliberate manipulation of search engine indexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doorway_page), a practice search engines have been explicitly working against since well before "local SEO" was a phrase anyone used. The specific risk to a small service business today — thin, swapped-variable city pages — is a direct descendant of that older, cruder pattern, just dressed in a more modern template.

It's also worth knowing this isn't confined to classic search anymore. Google has recently clarified that these same spam policies now explicitly extend to its AI-generated search results, with the stated reasoning being [to make it clear that the spam policies apply to all of Google Search, including generative AI responses](https://ppc.land/google-spam-policies-now-officially-cover-ai-overviews-and-ai-mode-in-search/). A page built as a doorway doesn't just risk ranking poorly in a list of ten blue links anymore. It risks being filtered out of whatever AI-generated summary a searcher sees instead.

## The actual test: four questions, not a word count

The mistake most businesses make when trying to self-diagnose is reaching for the wrong metric — word count, number of pages, keyword density — when none of those are what the policy is actually evaluating. The real test is a set of honest questions, and they're worth sitting with page by page rather than answering reflexively for the whole set at once.

**Would a visitor learn anything from this page they couldn't learn from the homepage?** Not "does it say the town name more times" — does it contain a fact, a job, a detail, a number that is actually true of that specific place and not true of the next town over. If every sentence on the page would still be accurate with the city name deleted, the page isn't doing the job it exists to do.

**Could this page be regenerated by changing one variable?** If the honest answer is "yes, swap the city name and the page is done," that's close to a definition of the problem, not a side effect of it. A real service-area page should break if you tried to do that — something on it (a project reference, a drive-time note, a locally specific detail) should stop making sense in a different town.

**Is the page reachable from the rest of the site, or does it exist as an island?** Pages that only exist to be landed on from search, with no link to them from the main navigation or the relevant service page, read as built for a crawler rather than a visitor — which is close to the literal definition of the problem.

**Has anything happened on this page in the last year?** A town page with no new project added, no review referencing that specific place, and no real search traffic in Search Console is, functionally, a page built once for ranking purposes and then abandoned — which is a reasonable working definition of a page that was never really about the visitor to begin with.

A page that fails more than one of these isn't a borderline case. It's the pattern the policy is written to catch, regardless of how good the writing on it is.

## How many pages, and which towns actually earn one

Once the test above is clear, the sizing question answers itself: build a page for a town because you have something specific to say about it, not because it sits inside your delivery radius. For most single-location service businesses, that's a short list — the handful of towns that produce the bulk of real jobs, real reviews, and real search volume — not the full roster of everywhere a truck could technically reach within Google's own [roughly 2 hours of driving time](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en) boundary.

Everywhere else you legitimately serve but don't have enough to say about individually belongs on one honest hub page — a single "areas we serve" page listing the full coverage area plainly, rather than forcing each town through its own thin template. That hub does three jobs at once: it tells visitors the truth about your coverage, it gives Google one clean, legitimate page to associate with the broader area, and it removes the temptation to pad out a dozen near-duplicate pages just to "cover" towns you rarely actually work in.

Internal structure matters here more than it gets credit for. The pages that do earn their own URL should be linked from the relevant service pages and from that hub, not left to exist only as search landing pages nobody on the actual site would ever click into. A page a real visitor can't find by browsing the site is a page that looks built only for search traffic — which is the exact appearance the policy is watching for.

## Where relevance, distance, and prominence fit into this

It's worth understanding why Google cares this much about geographic pages specifically, and the answer traces back to how local search ranking actually works underneath. Google's own guidance, summarized clearly in reporting on the topic, states plainly: [local results are based primarily on relevance, distance, and prominence. A combination of these factors helps us find the best match for your search. For example, our algorithms might decide that a business that's farther away from your location is more likely to have what you're looking for than a business that's closer, and therefore rank it higher in local results.](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/ranking-factors/relevance-distance-prominence/)

A thin, swapped-city doorway page is an attempt to manufacture relevance artificially — to tell Google's systems "we match this town" without actually demonstrating it through real content, real reviews naming the place, or real prominence signals tied to that specific area. Google's local algorithm isn't naive about this pattern precisely because it's common; thin geographic pages built purely to claim relevance are one of the most well-worn tricks in local search, which is exactly why the doorway policy exists in the form it does. A real service-area page earns relevance the slow way — genuine content specific to the place — rather than trying to claim it the fast way.

## A worked example: two pages for the same town

It helps to see the difference stated concretely rather than abstractly. Picture a roofing company, based in one location, that serves a neighboring town thirty minutes away.

The doorway version of that page opens with a sentence like "Looking for a trusted roofer in [Town]? Our team provides expert roofing services to homeowners throughout the area." It lists the same five services listed on every other page on the site, in the same order, with the town name swapped into two or three sentences. It ends with the same generic call to action every other page ends with. Nothing on it is false, exactly. Nothing on it is true in a way that's specific to that town, either.

The legitimate version opens by naming something actually true about that town — maybe it's an older neighborhood with a specific roofing material common to homes built in a certain decade, or a town with a specific permitting quirk that affects timelines, or simply a specific number of completed jobs there with a couple named streets or subdivisions. It references an actual project. It states a real response time based on actual drive distance from the shop, not a generic "fast response" claim. If a review exists naming that town, it's quoted or linked. None of this requires more design, more budget, or more technical sophistication than the doorway version. It requires someone who actually knows the business sitting down and writing down true, specific things — which is also, not coincidentally, exactly why so few businesses bother doing it the right way. It's slower than filling in a template.

## Fixing pages that already look like doorways

If an honest pass through the four questions above turns up pages that clearly fail, the fix isn't necessarily deletion — it's consolidation, and it's worth doing deliberately rather than reflexively. Pages representing towns with genuinely thin real-world activity should be folded into the single honest hub page described earlier, with the old URLs redirected rather than left to sit indexed and ignored. Pages representing towns with real, demonstrable activity — actual completed jobs, actual reviews — are worth keeping, but only once they're rewritten to pass the test: specific, checkable, non-interchangeable content, linked into the site's real navigation rather than floating as an orphaned landing page.

This is also where the GBP side needs a second look, independent of the website cleanup. Keeping the declared service area in your profile roughly aligned with where the website's real content and real job history actually live isn't a cosmetic detail — a profile claiming coverage the website can't back up with any real evidence is a mismatch Google's systems are, by design, built to notice.

## Where this actually leaves you

A service-area page is a genuinely useful piece of a small service business's website, and the instinct to build one isn't wrong. What's wrong, almost every time this goes badly, is treating the page as a template exercise — a city name dropped into a form letter twenty-two times — rather than as a real page that has to earn its own existence the same way any other page on the site does.

The test isn't complicated, even if applying it honestly takes more work than copy-pasting a template: would a visitor learn something true and specific to that place, could the page survive having its one variable changed, can it actually be found by browsing the site, and has anything happened on it recently that proves it's still connected to real work. A page that passes all four isn't a doorway with a different name on it. It's the thing a service-area page was supposed to be in the first place — and it's also, not coincidentally, the only version of this that actually produces a phone call instead of a Search Console warning.
